## Slide 1: Why booked meeting rooms sit empty at Harborview, from six interviews and two weeks of usage data

**On the slide:**

- Meeting Room Booking Study, S-01
- Maya Alvarez, readout of 2026-10-02

**Speaker notes:** This readout covers the Meeting Room Booking Study at Harborview Group
headquarters: six interviews held on 2026-09-15 to 2026-09-17 and a pull of room usage data for
2026-09-08 to 2026-09-19. All names and data are fictional.

## Slide 2: We interviewed six people and checked two weeks of usage for all 8 rooms

**On the slide:**

- 6 interviews: office manager, project manager, software engineer, sales representative, executive
  assistant, new hire
- Usage data: bookings and door-sensor occupancy, 8 rooms, 10 working days
- Questions: what are the pain points, how do people work around them, what surprised us

**Speaker notes:** The office manager had reported that rooms look booked but are empty. We chose six
people who use the rooms in different ways. We asked three questions: what the main pain points are
when booking, how people get around them, and what we did not expect. The usage data covers all 8
rooms over 10 working days.

## Slide 3: About 31% of booked room hours went unused (109 of 352 hours)

**On the slide:**

- 109 of 352 booked hours had no one in the room (31.0%)
- 78 of 268 bookings (29.1%) were no-shows: no one detected in the first 15 minutes
- Source: A-01

**Speaker notes:** Over the 10 working days, 352 hours were booked and 243 were used, leaving 109
unused, which is 31.0%. 78 of 268 bookings had no one in the room within the first 15 minutes, which
is 29.1%. This is the scale of the problem the office manager described. The data does not say why.

## Slide 4: Four pain points came up; booked-but-empty rooms was the most common (5 of 6 people)

**On the slide:**

| Pain point | People | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Booked rooms sit empty | 5 of 6 | F-01 |
| Room size and equipment not shown | 4 of 6 | F-02 |
| Booking and calendar invite are separate steps | 4 of 6 (1 more avoids RoomBook) | F-03 |
| Recurring bookings cannot be released one date at a time | 3 of 6 | F-04 |

**Speaker notes:** Five of six people had walked up to a booked room and found it empty (F-01). Four
said RoomBook does not show seats or equipment, and two of them had booked a room that was too small
(F-02). Four book in RoomBook and then add the room to the Outlook invite by hand, and a fifth
avoids RoomBook (F-03). Three hold recurring bookings that can only be cancelled as a whole series
(F-04). With six interviews these counts show the kinds of problems that exist, not how common they
are across the company.

## Slide 5: People work around every pain point by hand

**On the slide:**

- Empty rooms: look in the window, ping the booker in Teams, check the floor 3 tablet, or take the
  room anyway
- Missing room details: a Teams cheat sheet kept by the office manager, or ask someone
- Calendar gap: type the room name into the invite, or skip RoomBook and ask the office manager
- Recurring series: leave skipped dates booked, or cancel and rebook

**Speaker notes:** For empty rooms, P02 and P03 look in first, P03 and P05 message the booker, P05
trusts the live-occupancy tablet on floor 3, and P02 and P04 take an empty room. The office manager,
P01, keeps a pinned Teams message with each room's seats and screen, updated when someone
complains. For the calendar gap, P02, P03 and P06 type the room name into the invite, P05 updates
calendars herself, and P04 asks P01 to book for her. For recurring series, P01 and P02 leave skipped
dates booked, and P05 cancels and rebooks.

## Slide 6: Insight: people book rooms they may not need because getting one back is hard

**On the slide:**

- 4 of 6 book "just in case" (P02, P03, P05, P06); the office manager says "everyone" does
- "If I cancel, I lose it. Someone grabs it in minutes." (P05)
- A new hire learned it in week one: "I book two and cancel one." (P06)
- Our interpretation, not tested: empty bookings cause distrust, distrust causes defensive booking,
  which causes more empty bookings

**Speaker notes:** This was the result we did not expect. We expected people to describe other people's
empty bookings. Instead four of six described their own: holding a room until they know they do not
need it, booking a small room as backup, or booking two rooms and cancelling one. The new hire
picked up the habit from a colleague in the first weeks. The loop on the slide is our reading, not
something participants said, and we did not test it. See F-05.

## Slide 7: Small rooms are booked and left empty most: 40.4% of their booked hours were unused

**On the slide:**

| Room size | Unused share of booked hours |
|---|---|
| Small (4 seats) | 40.4% |
| Medium (6 seats) | 30.0% |
| Large (10 to 16 seats) | 25.0% |

- Highest: Keel, 44.1%. Lowest: Bridge, 18.4%.

**Speaker notes:** The three 4-seat rooms (Harbor, Anchor, Keel) had 42 of 104 booked hours unused,
which is 40.4%. The 6-seat rooms had 30 of 100 unused (30.0%) and the larger rooms 37 of 148 (25.0%).
Keel had the highest unused share at 44.1% and Bridge the lowest at 18.4%. This fits one
participant's habit of booking a small room as backup, but it does not prove it.

## Slide 8: Ideas to explore and open questions (not decisions)

**On the slide:**

- Ideas: show seats and equipment in the room list; write bookings to the calendar; let people
  release one date of a recurring series; release a booking automatically if no one arrives in 15
  minutes
- Open questions: would automatic release feel safe enough to stop defensive booking; how common are
  these problems across all staff; how often is the wrong room used

**Speaker notes:** These ideas come straight from the pain points and are not decisions or
commitments. The one most likely to change behavior is automatic release, because defensive booking
exists since people cannot easily get a room back. The open questions could not be answered with
six interviews and two weeks of data.

## Slide 9: What this study can and cannot tell us

**On the slide:**

- Six interviews at one site: shows kinds of problems, not how common they are
- Usage data shows that rooms go unused, not why
- All data in this repository is fictional

**Speaker notes:** Six participants cannot represent about 400 staff. The sensors detect presence, so
someone who stepped out of a booked room still counts as using it. The interview notes (N-01) are
the source for reasons, and the usage summary (A-01) for the numbers.
